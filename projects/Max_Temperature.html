A question for the evidence

Does sharing help every station?

Not consistently.

Original pooling beats local in Dallas–Fort Worth, but not Central Park or LAX. FedAvg has higher error than local at all three stations. With added NBS inputs, local has lower error than pooled at every station.

How was this comparison made?

Forecasts were issued at 18:00 local time on the day before the target date, using archived IEM forecasts and NOAA daily observations. The target is each station’s midnight-to-midnight local-standard-time maximum—not a citywide average.

Forecast availability
A six-hour forecast-arrival delay was assumed. Archived records establish that a forecast exists now, not exactly when it arrived historically.
Learning over time
Labels became eligible seven days after the climate-day end, by assumption. Models refit weekly on eligible past labels, then predicted the following week.
Earlier inspection
These assessment dates had already been examined in earlier work. Chronological fitting prevents future-label training, but does not make the comparison an untouched holdout.
Reading the experiments
Original local, pooled, and simulated FedAvg models share a linear residual architecture. The second group changes both the fitting approach and the available forecast inputs; it does not isolate a same-input improvement from sharing.

From weather records to forecasts

The project explores whether statistical post-processing can improve a station's next-day maximum-temperature forecast. It combines archived forecasts from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet with daily observations from NOAA, aligning forecast issue times, station records, and the day being predicted.

Weather features capture temperature trajectories, wind, cloud cover, and differences between forecast sources. Chronological validation keeps model selection ordered in time, while regional experiments compare local, pooled, and simulated federated models across the three stations.
